The Six-Day Game

A complete, published, commercially-shaped video game — ten worlds, fifty missions, a full progression economy — built in six working days by one person directing an AI. The interesting result is not the game. It is what the six days say about how fast a capable person can now move from idea to shipped product.

What was actually built

Not a prototype or a vertical slice. A finished, playable product, live on the public web and packaged as a native macOS application, with an automated test suite, a mutation-testing workflow, and roughly eleven thousand lines of engineering documentation explaining why each decision was taken.

Why a game is the right test case

Most AI coding demonstrations produce something with one system in it — a form, a dashboard, a script. They prove the model can write code. They do not prove it can hold a product together.

A game of this shape is unusually demanding because it is many interacting systems that must all remain true at once: a real-time physics simulation, procedurally generated terrain, an enemy AI with fairness guarantees, an economy, three progression systems, ten distinct environments with their own hazards, controller and touch input, audio synthesis, and save migration across forty iterations of the data format.

Change one number and five systems shift. That is precisely the condition under which naive AI output falls apart — and precisely the condition most real business software actually lives in. It is a harder test than it looks, and a more representative one.

The time compression

Before AI, a single skilled developer would need somewhere in the region of nine to eighteen months to reach this state: designed, built, balanced, tested, documented, packaged and shipped.

The arithmetic supports the estimate independently. Sustained professional output — including design, review, testing, rework and documentation — typically lands between twenty and fifty lines of retained code per developer-day. At that rate, 25,600 lines alone brackets 500–1,300 person-days before a single mission is authored or balanced.

Six days against nine to eighteen months is a compression of roughly fifty to a hundred times. Not an efficiency gain. A change in what one person can decide to attempt on a Monday.

It only worked because the operator was skilled

This is the part that does not survive being left out. The AI wrote effectively all of the code. It did not, and could not, supply the judgement — and the project's own history is the evidence.

Every consequential bug was found by the human, not by the 2,400 automated assertions. A crash that froze the game whenever the player's weapon destroyed anything had shipped to production and passed every test. A soundscape that measured correctly and sounded wrong. A weapon whose displayed blast radius was nearly twice its real one. The tests were green throughout.

Every consequential design decision was the human's, and several overruled the AI. Cutting the skill trees from thirty nodes to fifteen. Giving the player two equipment slots instead of one, because playtesting showed everyone made the same forced choice. Deferring features that were specified but not yet earned.

“This is not good design to have the slots twice. You should be able to mark the two active in one area.”

Product direction that no test could have produced

An unskilled operator would have accepted the green test suite, shipped the crash, kept the thirty nodes because the specification said thirty, and never noticed the sound. The speed comes from the AI. The direction, taste, and the ability to recognise a plausible-looking wrong answer come from the person.

What this means beyond games

Nothing about this is specific to games. The same pattern applies to any domain with real complexity and real users: internal tooling, customer-facing applications, data products, automation, analysis.

High-agency people with domain knowledge can now go from idea to MVP to shipped product in days rather than quarters — provided they are given the tools and the leeway to do it.

Both halves of that condition matter. The tools are the cheaper half. The leeway — permission to start without a business case, to ship without a committee, to throw away what does not work — is the half organisations usually withhold, and the half that determines whether any of this speed is realised.

The honest caveat: the ceiling is set by the operator, not the model. Given the same six days and the same tools, someone without the skill to interrogate the output would have produced something that looked finished and was not. This capability multiplies judgement; it does not substitute for it.
